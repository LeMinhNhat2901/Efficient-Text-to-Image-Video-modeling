A few years ago, if you wanted an image, you needed a camera, a designer, or a lot of patience.

Today, you type a sentence.

And somehow, a machine turns that sentence into pixels.

Not just random pixels — but structured, colorful, meaningful images.

Welcome back to our journey through generative artificial intelligence.

In the previous part, through Richard Hartley’s lecture, we looked closely at the mathematics of diffusion models: the equations, the noise process, and the reverse path from chaos back to structure.

But today, we take one step back.

Instead of zooming into one equation, we zoom out and look at the full map.

Based on Srikumar Ramalingam’s talk from Google Research, this chapter is about the cornerstones of the Text-to-Pixels journey.

The big question is simple:

How does language — something abstract, symbolic, and human — become an image made of millions of numbers?

The answer is not one model.

It is a chain of ideas: embeddings, visual words, Transformers, diffusion, latent spaces, and efficient inference.

So let’s follow the path.

From text…

to meaning…

to pixels.

Let’s begin with the magic trick.

A single line of text can generate a robot cooking in a kitchen.

A robot painted as graffiti on a brick wall.

A raccoon wearing formal clothes and a top hat.

Or even a hyper-realistic alien pyramid landscape.

And the strange part is not just that these images exist.

The strange part is how natural it now feels.

We type. The model imagines.

But this story does not stop at static images.

Once a system can generate key images from text, those images can become building blocks for video. Generate keyframes first, then refine motion with temporal and spatial super-resolution.

And with the right 3D representation, text-to-image can also support text-to-3D systems, where image generation and NeRF-like representations work together to build objects in space.

So text-to-image is not just one application.

It is a centerpiece.

It sits at the center of image generation, video generation, 3D generation, editing, super-resolution, and many other creative systems.

But if we peel away the beautiful outputs and look underneath, most conditional generation pipelines rely on two major families of methods.

The first is autoregressive generation.

Build the output step by step, token by token — much like a language model predicts the next word.

The second is diffusion-based generation.

Start from noise, then gradually recover structure through a learned denoising process.

One family builds.

The other family cleans.

And both need the same missing ingredient:

a way to connect human language with visual meaning.

Because before a model can generate an image from text, it must first understand where that text lives in the visual world.

The rise of text-to-image generation was not a single invention.

It was a puzzle assembled over many years.

U-Net and diffusion gave us powerful tools for reconstruction and denoising.

Transformers gave us a way to model long-range dependencies.

VQGAN helped turn images into discrete visual tokens.

DALL-E and latent diffusion models showed how these pieces could become real generative systems.

But one puzzle piece is especially important for connecting text and image:

CLIP.

Before CLIP, vision models were already good at learning feature spaces.

Images containing similar objects — or similar semantic structure — tend to live close to each other.

A dog, a wolf, and another dog photographed from a different angle may not be identical images, but their features are related. In embedding space, they begin to form neighborhoods.

That is already powerful.

But image features alone are not enough.

Text still lives in another universe.

The phrase “autonomous car” is not an image.

It has no pixels, no edges, no texture, no color.

So the question becomes:

How do we place language and vision into the same mathematical space?

CLIP and ALIGN solve this with an image-text co-embedding space.

One encoder maps images into vectors.

Another encoder maps text into vectors.

During training, matching image-text pairs are pulled closer together, while mismatched pairs are pushed apart.

This is the idea behind contrastive learning.

Inside each batch, the model compares every image with every text.

The correct pairs sit on the diagonal of the similarity matrix.

Training pushes those diagonal scores up, and pushes the wrong matches down.

Over time, language becomes a coordinate system for vision.

A prompt is no longer just a string of characters.

It becomes an address.

Not an image yet — but an address pointing to where an image should live in visual space.

And that is the first cornerstone.

The model now knows where the prompt belongs.

But it still has not created a single pixel.

So the next question is even more important:

How should an image be represented so that a machine can actually write it out?

That brings us to visual words, image tokenization, and VQGAN.

In the previous section, we gave text a place in visual space.

A prompt became an address.

But an address is not a building.

To generate an image, the model still needs a way to actually construct pixels.

And here comes the next question:

What is the “vocabulary” of an image?

Language models generate sentences by predicting tokens. One word, then the next word, then the next.

So a tempting idea appears:

Can we do the same thing for images?

The problem is that images do not come with words pre-installed.

An image is a continuous field of pixels. Hundreds of thousands, sometimes millions, of values.

Asking a model to predict every pixel directly is possible in theory, but in practice, it is painfully expensive — like writing a novel one molecule of ink at a time.

So instead of looking at individual pixels, we look at small visual regions.

A dog’s eye.

A nose.

A patch of fur.

The edge of a car.

A piece of road texture.

Each small part carries information.

This is the intuition behind visual words.

If we collect many image patches and place them into feature space, similar patches begin to form clusters.

The center of each cluster becomes a prototype.

A visual word.

So instead of representing an image as raw pixels, we can describe it using a dictionary of reusable visual patterns.

This idea also connects image search and image generation.

If we can describe an image well enough with visual words, we are already one step closer to reconstructing it — or generating a new one.

VQGAN takes this idea and turns it into a powerful architecture.

A 256 by 256 RGB image goes into a CNN encoder.

The encoder compresses it into a smaller spatial grid of continuous features.

Then, at each location in that grid, the model looks into a codebook — a learned dictionary of visual vectors — and finds the nearest entry.

That nearest entry becomes a discrete token.

Not one token for the whole image, but one token for each position in the latent grid.

The result is a token image: a small matrix of integers.

In other words, the image has been translated from the continuous world of pixels into the discrete world of tokens.

And this is the key move.

Once an image becomes tokens, image generation starts to look much more like language modeling.

But VQGAN is not just a tokenizer.

It combines two strengths.

CNNs are excellent at learning local, context-rich visual parts.

Transformers are excellent at modeling long-range interactions between those parts.

That makes VQGAN a useful bridge between vision and sequence modeling, and a backbone for many conditional generation systems.

Training happens in two broad stages.

First, the model learns the encoder, the decoder, and the codebook.

The encoder compresses the image.

The codebook quantizes the representation.

The decoder reconstructs the image.

Then, in the second stage, a Transformer can learn to synthesize these image tokens, possibly conditioned on text, depth, edges, semantic maps, or other signals.

But for the codebook to work, the loss function has to do several jobs at once.

The reconstruction loss makes sure the decoded image still looks like the original.

The codebook and commitment losses pull the encoder outputs and codebook vectors toward each other, so quantization becomes stable.

And because plain reconstruction can produce blurry results, VQGAN also uses perceptual and GAN losses.

The discriminator acts like a visual judge.

It asks: does this reconstructed image merely have the right numbers, or does it actually look real?

The result is a major turning point.

An image is no longer just a giant wall of pixels.

It becomes a compact sequence of visual tokens.

And once images become tokens, we can hand them to one of the most powerful machines in modern AI:

Transformers.

Now that images have become tokens, we face a new problem.

Who is going to model the relationships between them?

Because an image is not just a bag of patches.

The eye depends on the face.

The shadow depends on the light.

A tiny corner of the image may depend on an object far away.

Computer vision has been moving toward this problem for decades.

At one stage, Markov Random Fields modeled images through local interactions.

Each variable in a grid only talked to its neighbors.

The model minimized an energy function: unary costs for individual labels, and pairwise costs for neighboring compatibility.

It was local, structured, and elegant.

Then came CNNs.

CNNs became the workhorse of vision because they are very good at extracting local features hierarchically.

Edges become textures.

Textures become object parts.

Object parts become larger visual structures.

But images are not only local.

Sometimes, a patch in the top-left corner needs to communicate with a patch in the bottom-right corner.

The color of the sky affects the lighting on the car.

The position of one eye affects the position of the other.

This is where Transformers become important.

With attention, one token can directly interact with another token far away.

Vision Transformers take an image, split it into patches, turn each patch into a token, add positional embeddings, and pass the whole sequence through Transformer layers.

With VQGAN, this becomes especially powerful.

The encoder, decoder, and codebook turn image synthesis into a sequence prediction problem.

A Transformer can now generate image tokens.

And if we provide a condition — text, depth, semantic maps, edge maps, or a low-resolution image — the model can synthesize an image guided by that condition.

So at first, it sounds simple.

We have visual tokens.

We have Transformers.

Just generate the next token, right?

Well… almost.

There is one very annoying problem.

Images are two-dimensional.

Language has a natural order.

Word one comes before word two.

The sentence moves forward.

But an image is a grid.

There is no obvious “first patch” and “next patch.”

Should we scan from left to right?

Top to bottom?

Spiral inward?

Shuffle the patches?

Use some clever space-filling curve?

Every ordering imposes a bias.

And if we force a two-dimensional image into a one-dimensional sequence, the model may have to learn awkward dependencies that were never awkward in the image itself.

This is the ordering problem.

It reminds us that image generation is not simply “GPT, but for pictures.”

Images have spatial structure.

And the way we choose to linearize that structure affects training, inference speed, and image quality.

So the next challenge is clear:

Can we keep the power of token-based generation, without paying the full price of slow sequential decoding?

The ordering problem makes sequential image generation slow.

If a model has to fill the image token by token, one step after another, it can feel like watching a printer from 1998.

Accurate, maybe.

Fast? Not exactly.

So models like MaskGIT and Muse take a different route.

Instead of predicting tokens in one fixed sequence, they mask many tokens and recover them through several rounds of parallel decoding.

The image starts rough.

Then the model fills in many missing parts at once.

Then it refines again.

And again.

This is much faster than strict left-to-right generation.

Muse is an important example of this idea: a masked generative Transformer for text-to-image generation.

According to the slide, the Muse 3B model is about ten times faster than Parti or Imagen 3B on TPUv4.

That is a serious speedup.

But speed usually comes with a catch.

When we reduce the number of decoding steps, the image may lose local consistency.

Some tokens may not agree with their neighbors.

The global idea may be right, but a few local regions can look slightly off — the visual equivalent of wearing a tuxedo with flip-flops.

This is where MarkovGen brings an old idea back into the room.

Markov Random Fields.

Instead of asking the Transformer to do all the work, MarkovGen treats the token image as a structured prediction problem.

Each token is a variable.

Neighboring tokens are connected by local constraints.

And the goal is to find a token assignment that is both likely according to the model and consistent across the image.

The energy function has two main parts.

The first is the unary cost.

This cost penalizes a token if its selected label disagrees with what the model predicted.

In simple terms: if the classifier says, “this token probably should not be label 42,” then choosing label 42 should be expensive.

The second is the pairwise cost.

This cost penalizes neighboring tokens if they are incompatible.

For example, two adjacent regions should usually have reasonable continuity in texture, object boundaries, or local semantic structure.

If two neighboring tokens clash, the energy goes up.

Then MarkovGen uses MRF inference to minimize this energy and refine the token image.

It is not replacing the generative model.

It is acting like a structured clean-up step — a final pass that says, “Wait, these local pieces should agree with each other.”

And this is the beautiful part.

A classical idea from computer vision becomes useful again inside a modern text-to-image system.

The result is a speedup over Muse without quality loss.

In the table from the slide, Muse takes about 442 milliseconds, while MarkovGen reduces this to about 281 milliseconds.

So the story is not just “new models replace old ideas.”

The better story is:

old ideas return when the problem needs structure.

MRFs, CNNs, Transformers, codebooks, and masked decoding are not isolated inventions.

They are pieces of the same machine.

And by this point, we have followed one major road: image tokenization plus Transformer-based generation.

But text-to-image has another road — the one that dominates many modern systems.

Instead of filling tokens, it begins with noise.

And then learns how to recover structure.

Next stop:

Diffusion models.

So far, we have followed one road:

turn the image into tokens, then let a Transformer generate those tokens.

Now, we take the second road.

The road that begins with something that looks completely useless:

noise.

Diffusion starts with an idea that feels almost backwards.

Instead of learning how to build an image from scratch, we first learn how to destroy one.

Slowly.

Carefully.

Systematically.

Take this clean image of a cat.

At the first step, we add just a little Gaussian noise.

At the next step, a little more.

Then more.

Edges begin to disappear.

Textures fade.

Shapes dissolve.

And after enough steps, the image becomes almost pure static — like a broken television trying very hard to be modern art.

This is the forward diffusion process.

And the nice thing about this process is that we designed it.

We know exactly how much noise we added.

The real challenge is the reverse direction.

How do we go from random noise back to something structured?

How do we travel from chaos… back to a cat?

Here is the important trick.

In this formulation, the neural network is not directly trying to paint the clean image in one shot.

It is not sitting there thinking, “Ah yes, one perfect cat, coming right up.”

Instead, it learns to predict the noise.

We give the model a noisy image, x_t, together with the timestep t.

The model outputs an estimate of the noise that was added.

If that estimate is good, we can subtract part of the noise away, and the image becomes slightly cleaner.

Not perfect.

Just slightly cleaner.

And that is enough.

Because if we repeat this many times, small improvements accumulate.

Noise becomes shape.

Shape becomes structure.

Structure becomes image.

The loss function is beautifully simple: compare the true noise epsilon with the predicted noise from the model.

The closer they are, the better the model becomes at denoising.

So during training, we start from a clean image, add noise, and ask the model to predict that noise.

During sampling, we do the opposite.

We start from Gaussian noise and repeatedly remove what the model thinks is noise.

Step by step, the static begins to organize itself.

Diffusion, in this sense, is not just image generation.

It is the art of learning how to reverse destruction.

The intuition is simple:

add noise, then learn how to remove it.

But to implement diffusion, we need to turn that intuition into mathematics.

The forward process is a Markov chain.

We start with a clean image, x_0, sampled from the data distribution.

Then we create x_1, x_2, all the way to x_T, by adding Gaussian noise step by step.

At every timestep, the transition has two jobs.

First, it weakens the original signal.

That is the square root of one minus beta_t, multiplied by x_{t minus one}.

Second, it adds new Gaussian noise, controlled by beta_t.

So beta_t is the noise schedule.

It decides how aggressively we corrupt the image at each timestep.

Early steps usually add only a small amount of noise.

Later steps make noise dominate more and more, until the original image structure almost disappears.

Geometrically, the mean is pulled toward zero, while the noise becomes stronger.

If the total amount of noise is large enough, x_T becomes approximately a sample from a standard Gaussian distribution.

In other words, after enough corruption, every image ends up looking like noise.

Very democratic.

But there is a practical problem.

During training, we do not want to run hundreds or thousands of forward steps just to create one noisy image at timestep t.

That would be painfully slow.

Luckily, the math gives us a shortcut.

We define alpha_t as one minus beta_t.

Then alpha_bar_t is the product of all alpha values from step one to step t.

With this single quantity, we can sample x_t directly from x_0.

No long loop.

No slow walk through time.

Just a jump.

The formula says:

x_t equals square root of alpha_bar_t times x_0, plus square root of one minus alpha_bar_t times epsilon.

This is the time jump that makes training efficient.

Now the training objective becomes very clean.

We sample an image x_0.

We sample a timestep t.

We sample noise epsilon.

We construct x_t using the shortcut.

Then we feed x_t and t into the neural network, epsilon_theta, and ask it to predict epsilon.

The loss is simply the squared error between the true noise and the predicted noise.

That is the simple diffusion loss.

The training pseudocode is basically a loop:

sample an image,

sample a timestep,

sample noise,

create a noisy image,

update the model to predict the noise.

Sampling runs in the opposite direction.

Start from x_T, sampled from a standard Gaussian.

Then loop backward from T to one.

At each step, predict the noise and take one reverse step toward a cleaner image.

At the end of the loop, what began as pure noise becomes an image with structure.

That is the mathematical engine of diffusion:

a carefully designed destruction process,

and a learned path back.

Now we know how diffusion can turn noise into an image.

But there is still a problem.

Noise is too free.

The same random cloud could become a cat, a lion, a leopard, or something that looks like all three had a very confusing family reunion.

So how do we make the sampling process go in the direction we want?

This is where guidance enters the story.

Guidance is the steering wheel of diffusion.

Without guidance, the model may generate a plausible image.

With guidance, we can push the reverse process toward a specific class, a specific prompt, or a specific visual meaning.

The first idea is classifier guidance.

We use an additional image classifier, trained to recognize images even when they are noisy.

At every denoising step, the classifier looks at x_t and asks:

Which direction would make this image more likely to belong to the desired class?

If the label is “cats,” the classifier provides a gradient that nudges the reverse diffusion path toward cat-like images.

Mathematically, we take the model’s original denoising direction and add a guidance vector from the classifier.

That vector is multiplied by a scale factor, s.

This scale controls the tradeoff.

If s is small, the image may be more diverse, but less controlled.

If s is large, the image follows the condition more strongly, but it may become less natural or less varied.

So guidance scale is a steering wheel and a gas pedal at the same time.

Useful, but dangerous if you floor it.

The downside of classifier guidance is that it needs an extra classifier.

And not just any classifier — one that works on noisy images.

That is inconvenient.

Classifier-free guidance solves this in a more elegant way.

Instead of using an external classifier, we use the same diffusion model twice.

During training, sometimes the model receives the condition, such as a label or text prompt.

Sometimes the condition is dropped.

So at sampling time, the model can make two predictions.

One prediction with the condition:

f of x_t and y.

And one prediction without the condition:

f of x_t and empty.

The difference between these two predictions tells us what the condition is doing.

It tells us the direction that moves the sample from “generic image” toward “image matching the condition.”

Then we multiply that difference by the guidance scale s.

This is classifier-free guidance.

A good way to think about it is this:

the model asks itself two questions.

Where would I go without the prompt?

And where would I go with the prompt?

The difference between those answers becomes the steering direction.

Finally, CLIP guidance brings language back into the center of the story.

Instead of using a simple label like “cats,” we can use a richer text prompt, such as:

a cyberpunk cat.

CLIP maps the prompt into the shared image-text embedding space.

The current image estimate is also mapped into that same space.

Then guidance tries to increase the similarity between the image vector and the text vector.

In other words, CLIP guidance pulls the sampling process toward images that better match the language prompt.

Classifier guidance, classifier-free guidance, and CLIP guidance all create steering signals in different ways.

But the goal is the same:

turn denoising from an unconditional process into conditional generation.

Diffusion gives us the road from noise to image.

Guidance decides which road we take.

And once we can generate the image we want, one question naturally comes next:

Can we do it faster?

Can we do it cheaper?

Can we make the whole process lighter?

That is why the next stop is Latent Diffusion.

Diffusion gives us a powerful way to turn noise into images.

But there is one very practical problem.

Pixel space is huge.

A 1024 by 1024 image with three color channels contains more than three million values.

And if every denoising step has to process all of them, the model is basically trying to paint a mural with a toothbrush.

Beautiful?

Maybe.

Efficient?

Absolutely not.

This is where Latent Diffusion makes a very clever move.

Do not run diffusion directly on pixels.

First, compress the image.

An encoder takes the image from pixel space and maps it into a smaller latent space.

This latent representation is not the full image in the raw pixel sense, but it still keeps the important information: layout, shape, texture, and semantic structure.

Now the diffusion process happens inside this smaller latent space.

Noise is added to the latent.

The denoising U-Net works on the latent.

The reverse process cleans the latent.

Not x, the full pixel image.

But z, the compressed representation.

That is a huge difference.

Because the space is smaller, each denoising step becomes cheaper, faster, and much more practical for high-resolution generation.

Conditions can also enter this process.

Text prompts, semantic maps, reference images, or other representations can guide the denoising network.

In latent diffusion models, cross-attention is the mechanism that lets the prompt talk to the latent image as it is being formed.

Once denoising is finished, the decoder maps the clean latent back into pixel space.

So the model does not dream directly in pixels.

It dreams in a compressed sketch.

Then it decodes the dream into an image.

But engineers have a very special personality trait:

when something becomes faster, they immediately ask,

“Can it be faster than faster?”

That brings us to LatentCRF.

Even in latent space, the denoising trajectory may still require many iterative steps.

LatentCRF asks a practical question:

If some of those steps are mainly refining the latent so it better matches the structure of natural image latents, can we replace several LDM inference iterations with a lighter CRF inference step?

The idea is to operate directly on latent vectors.

These latent vectors are not independent.

A latent vector should be compatible with its neighbors.

And sometimes, a whole group of latent vectors should work together to represent a larger structure.

Pairwise interactions check local consistency.

Do nearby latent vectors agree with each other?

Higher-order interactions go beyond pairs.

They ask whether a group of latents forms a coherent structure together.

So instead of repeatedly applying the U-Net for every small correction, LatentCRF uses structured inference to make the latent space more consistent.

The result shown in the slide is about 33 percent faster inference compared with Full LDM, with visually comparable quality on examples like peonies, a blue Porsche, a formal raccoon, and a cat portrait.

The important point is not just the number.

It is the pattern.

The model is no longer blindly repeating denoising steps.

It is exploiting structure.

Latent space makes diffusion lighter.

LatentCRF gives inference a shortcut.

And yet, the race is not over.

Because once we make the process faster, someone will always look at the GPU and say:

“Nice. Now do it again, but cheaper.”

At the beginning of this journey, we saw two major backbones for text-to-image generation.

One side was diffusion-based.

The other side was autoregressive.

Now, near the end, both sides are evolving toward the same goal:

faster generation,

lighter computation,

and better scaling.

On the diffusion side, we have SANA.

SANA represents a direction where high-resolution image synthesis is made more efficient with a Linear Diffusion Transformer.

Instead of relying only on the traditional U-Net-style backbone, SANA redesigns the denoising engine around efficiency.

The first key idea is deep compression.

Many latent diffusion systems compress the image by about 8 times.

SANA pushes this much further, using a 32 times deep compression autoencoder.

A smaller latent representation means fewer latent tokens.

Fewer tokens mean less computation.

And less computation means the GPU gets to breathe for once.

The second key idea is linear attention.

Traditional attention often scales quadratically with the number of tokens.

When the sequence length grows, the cost grows very quickly.

It is the kind of curve that looks harmless at first, and then suddenly tries to leave the planet.

Linear attention aims to make that cost grow much more gently.

This helps the model handle larger images more efficiently.

SANA also emphasizes Complex Human Instruction.

In other words, the model is not just trying to follow short prompts like “a cat.”

It is designed to handle richer, more detailed instructions.

Together with VLM-based image captioning, Linear Attention, and Mix-FFN blocks, the goal is clear:

high-resolution generation with less computational weight.

While SANA improves the diffusion side, VAR rethinks the autoregressive side.

Traditional autoregressive text generation predicts the next token.

Image autoregressive models often try to predict the next image token.

But VAR changes the question.

It does not ask:

“What is the next token?”

It asks:

“What should the next scale of the image look like?”

This is next-scale prediction.

The image begins at a very coarse resolution.

Then the model predicts the next, more detailed scale.

Then the next one.

And the next one.

The image becomes sharper step by step.

This is closer to how humans often draw.

First, a rough composition.

Then the main shapes.

Then object parts.

Then texture and fine details.

Instead of forcing a two-dimensional image into a long one-dimensional token sequence, VAR builds the image from coarse to fine.

Not next token.

Next scale.

SANA and VAR come from different directions.

One makes diffusion lighter with compression and linear attention.

The other rethinks autoregressive generation through scale.

But they are both answering the same engineering question:

How do we generate high-quality images faster, cheaper, and at larger scale?

And when these pieces are placed side by side, the final lesson becomes clear.

The future is not only about bigger models.

It is about smarter representations and more efficient paths through them.

Now that all the pieces are on the table, the final message becomes clear.

The question is not only:

Which model is stronger?

The deeper question is:

How do we represent images in the first place?

We started from a prompt.

Then we moved through co-embedding spaces, visual words, codebooks, Transformers, diffusion, guidance, latent spaces, and efficient inference.

The Text-to-Pixels journey is not one magical box.

It is a machine made of many layers.

And the first big lesson is this:

Image representation is the key.

If we represent images as raw pixels, the model has to work in a massive space.

Every detail is explicit.

Every color value is there.

Every denoising step is expensive.

But if we represent images as latent vectors or discrete tokens, we compress the important information.

We give the model a smaller, more structured space to work in.

That is why representation keeps appearing again and again in this story.

CLIP represents text and images in a shared embedding space.

VQGAN represents images as discrete tokens.

Latent diffusion represents images as compressed latent vectors.

VAR represents image generation as a sequence of scales.

Different representations open different doors.

The second lesson is that architecture still matters.

Diffusion models can be effective with U-Nets.

They can also be pushed toward Transformer-based architectures.

But architecture and representation work together.

A powerful model in a bad space may still be inefficient.

A smart representation can make the model’s job much easier.

The third lesson is more uncomfortable.

Beautiful generations are not free.

They come from larger datasets, more compute, and increasingly expensive training.

The slide reminds us of the scale jump from ImageNet, with about 1.2 million images, to LAION, with billions of images.

And behind those images are many GPU and TPU hours.

A lot of electricity.

A lot of hardware.

And probably at least one engineer staring at a training dashboard at 3 a.m. whispering,

“Please do not crash.”

Training is expensive.

But inference is the cost that repeats.

Every time a user types a prompt, the system has to generate again.

If millions of users do this, inference cost becomes a central problem.

That is why methods like MarkovGen, LatentCRF, SANA, and VAR are not small details.

They are part of making text-to-image generation practical.

Faster.

Cheaper.

More scalable.
