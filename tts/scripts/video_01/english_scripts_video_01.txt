Look at this puppy.

For now, it is perfectly recognizable.

Two eyes, a nose, fur, background - a tiny, adorable bundle of pixels.

Very scientific.

Very cute.

But now...

let us do something slightly cruel...

in the name of mathematics.

At every time step, we add a little bit of random noise.

Not too much at first.

Just enough to disturb the image.

Then a little more.

And more.

After many steps, the puppy disappears.

What remains is not a dog.

Not a face.

Not even a bad drawing.

It is just noise.

This is the easy direction.

If you add random noise to a structured object for long enough, structure breaks down.

It is the same intuition behind entropy:

when randomness keeps entering a system...

order tends to dissolve.

Now let us switch from pixels to geometry.

Instead of thinking of this image as a picture, imagine it as a point in a very high-dimensional space.

A small image may have thousands or millions of pixel values.

In machine learning language, an image is just one point in a huge coordinate system.

A very emotional point, if it is a puppy, but still a point.

For visualization, we compress this idea into two dimensions.

Suppose our clean data lives on a beautiful spiral-shaped structure.

This is the data distribution:

ordered, meaningful, and concentrated.

Now we add noise step by step.

The spiral begins to blur.

The points spread out.

The structure dissolves into a cloud.

This direction is called the forward process.

But diffusion models are built around the opposite question.

Can we start from pure noise...

and step by step...

move back toward structure?

Not necessarily to recover this exact original puppy - that would be too strong.

Instead, can we learn a path from random noise...

toward regions where realistic data is likely to live?

That learned backward process is the heart of diffusion models.

So the story of this video is simple:

First, we destroy structure in a controlled way.

Then, using probability, neural networks, and a surprising amount of beautiful mathematics...

we learn how to come back.


To make this destruction useful, we need to describe it mathematically.

Let X t be the state of our data at time step t.

The forward process says that X t is produced from the previous state X t minus one.

A simple version looks like this:

X t equals alpha t times X t minus one,

plus square root beta t times G.

This equation has two jobs.

The first term, alpha t times X t minus one, keeps some of the old signal.

Usually, alpha t is less than one, so it slightly shrinks the previous data.

In plain English: it keeps the old image, but makes it a bit weaker.

The second term, square root beta t times G, adds Gaussian noise.

Here, G is independent Gaussian noise.

The value beta t controls the noise variance, and square root beta t is the scale of the noise we actually add.

So at every step, we do two things: preserve a little signal, inject a little randomness.

If this sounds like carefully destroying your data, that is exactly what it is.

We are not smashing the image with a hammer.

We are dissolving it with a pipette.

Now look at the variance circle.

The horizontal direction represents how much clean structure remains.

The vertical direction represents how much noise we add.

As time increases, the vector rotates away from the structure axis and toward the noise axis.

The important idea is that the process is controlled.

We do not want the data to explode into infinity.

We want the signal to fade while the noise takes over in a mathematically predictable way.

This is why the random walks stay inside a growing but controlled band of uncertainty.

The trajectories look messy, but the distribution around them is not arbitrary.

And because Gaussian noise behaves so nicely, we get a shortcut.

Instead of simulating every tiny step from X zero to X t, we can jump directly:

X t equals alpha bar t times X zero,

plus square root beta bar t times G.

Here, alpha bar t summarizes all the signal-preserving factors from the first step up to time t.

This shortcut is extremely useful.

During training, we can choose any time step t, create a noisy version immediately,

and ask the model to learn how to undo it.

So the forward process is not the hard part.

We designed it.

The real challenge is the backward process.


Now let us look more carefully at the forward equation.

There is a very important property hiding inside it.

To produce X t, we only use X t minus one.

We do not directly look at X t minus two, or X zero, or the entire history of the system.

It is like a robot walking through a foggy maze with extremely short-term memory.

At each step, the robot only says: "Where am I now?

Great.

Let me move from here." It does not open a full autobiography of every previous step.

That would be dramatic, but unnecessary.

This is the Markov property.

It says:

Once we know the current state,

the distant past does not provide additional direct information about the next state.

More formally:

p of x t, given x zero through x t minus one,

equals p of x t, given x t minus one.

This does not mean the past is irrelevant in a philosophical sense.

The past helped create x t minus one.

But once x t minus one is known, that state already summarizes everything the process needs for the next step.

In the animation, all older nodes fade away.

A wall falls between the distant past and the present.

Only the arrow from x t minus one to x t remains bright.

This simple idea gives us a powerful factorization.

The probability of the entire trajectory can be written as:

p of x zero to T,

equals p of x zero,

times the product from t equals one to T,

of p of x t given x t minus one.

Instead of modeling one enormous transformation from clean data to pure noise, we model many small transitions.

And that is exactly why diffusion models are trainable.

They turn one impossible leap into a long sequence of tiny, manageable steps.


Now comes the key question.

If the forward Markov chain takes us from data to noise,

can a reverse Markov chain take us from noise back to data?

In symbols, can we describe transitions like this?

q of x t minus one, given x t.

In other words: if I am standing at a noisy state x t, what is the probability distribution over the previous,

slightly cleaner state x t minus one?

Theorem 2.2.5 says yes: the same joint distribution can be written in the reverse direction.

And the tool that makes this possible is Bayes' rule.

Forward probabilities and backward probabilities are connected.

If we expand the full trajectory probability using Bayes' rule, a beautiful cancellation happens.

At first, the expression looks terrifying - and mathematically, "terrifying" usually means "please wait,

something will cancel."

We get a long chain of fractions.

Terms like q of x one, q of x two, and so on appear in both numerators and denominators.

Then the telescoping begins.

One term cancels.

Then another.

Then another.

It is like watching a long line of dominoes politely remove themselves from the equation.

And after all the cancellations, the reverse factorization remains:

q of x zero to T,

equals q of x T,

times the product from t equals one to T,

of q of x t minus one, given x t.

This is a major turning point.

It means the path from noise back to data can also be described as a Markov chain.

But there is a catch - and in mathematics, the catch is usually where the PhD begins.

The formula tells us the reverse conditionals exist.

It does not tell us how to compute them in high-dimensional image space.

For a tiny toy dataset, maybe we can calculate everything.

But for a million-pixel image?

Good luck writing down the full probability density by hand.

You would need a very large notebook and possibly a snack.

So the next question is not: "Does the reverse process exist?"

It is:

How do we learn it?


Imagine being lost in a dark forest.

In the fairy tale, Hansel and Gretel try to find their way back using breadcrumbs.

In diffusion, we are also lost - but our forest is a high-dimensional cloud of noise.

And our breadcrumbs are not made of bread.

They are made of probability.

Now look at this probability terrain.

Bright regions are where data is likely to live.

Dark regions are low-probability noise.

In our two-dimensional toy example,

the data forms something like a Swiss roll - a structured region inside a much larger space.

A noisy point y falls into the dark region.

What direction should it move?

This is where the score function appears.

nabla log p X of y.

The score is a local compass.

At the current point y, it points in the direction where log-probability increases fastest.

Notice the word local.

The score does not magically draw a straight line all the way home.

It does not open Google Maps and say, "In 300 meters, turn left at the Gaussian."

It only tells us the best infinitesimal direction from where we are right now.

In the animation, one arrow appears at y.

Then nearby arrows pulse into view.

Finally, the whole vector field appears as a probability compass over the terrain.

If we take a tiny step along the score, the point moves toward a region of higher probability.

Repeat this many times, and the point begins to climb back toward the data structure.

This is the intuition behind the reverse process:

not one giant jump from noise to data, but many small probability-guided steps.

The score is the compass.

The data manifold is home.

And the noisy point is just trying not to get eaten by birds.



Now we need to turn this compass into an equation.

Globally, the probability landscape is complicated.

It bends, twists, folds, and generally behaves like a mathematical mountain range.

But locally, if we zoom into a tiny neighborhood around y, the landscape becomes much simpler.

This is the idea of local linear approximation.

Inside the magnifying glass, the curved contour lines start to look almost straight.

The complicated terrain becomes approximately a tilted plane.

And in that small neighborhood, the theorem gives us a beautiful approximation:

mu of X given y,

is approximately y plus beta times nabla log p X of y.

Let us unpack this.

mu of X given y is the conditional mean - the average previous state that could have produced the noisy point y.

The formula says:

Start at the current point y.

Look at the score direction.

Take a small step in that direction.

The result approximates the backward mean.

The coefficient beta controls the step size.

If beta is small, the step is short and stable.

The local approximation remains trustworthy.

If beta is too large, the point overshoots.

It jumps past the region where the local linear picture was valid.

In the animation, the stable step lands near the probability ridge,

while the oversized step flies off like a student who clicked "submit" without reading the question.

This is why diffusion uses many small steps.

Small steps are easier to approximate.

But now we hit the real wall.

The formula requires nabla log p X of y.

For a two-dimensional toy landscape, we can draw it.

For real images with millions of dimensions, computing this score explicitly is essentially impossible.

So the formula is beautiful.

But it hides the hardest part:

How do we get the score?

That is where learning enters.



Instead of explicitly computing the score everywhere, we train a neural network to solve a denoising problem.

Here is the statistical miracle.

Suppose we know a noisy observation y.

There may be many possible cleaner states x that could have produced it.

If the model must output one single prediction, what should it choose?

Let us visualize the possibilities as points.

Each possible x pulls on the prediction.

If the prediction is too far left, many distances become large.

If it is too far right, same problem.

The best compromise is the center of mass of all possible x's conditioned on y.

That center is:

the expected value of X, given Y equals y.

Now we turn this into training.

We define a squared error loss:

L of f theta,

equals the expected squared distance between f theta of y and x.

This is ordinary mean squared error.

Nothing fancy.

The kind of loss function that has been quietly doing homework since the early days of machine learning.

But the theorem says something powerful:

The function that minimizes this expected squared error is:

f star of y equals the expected value of X, given Y equals y.

So MSE does not just make predictions "close." Under ideal conditions, it learns the conditional mean.

In the animation, the balance beam finds its center.

Then it morphs into a parabola.

A ball rolls down to the minimum.

At the bottom, the formula appears:

f star of y equals the expected value of X, given Y equals y.

In diffusion, this becomes the learned backward mean:

mu theta of y and t.

This is very important.

We are not saying the neural network becomes perfect.

In practice, it learns an approximation.

The score compass is estimated, not magically downloaded from the universe.

But this approximation is enough.

Instead of computing the impossible probability density directly, the network learns how to denoise.

And by learning to denoise, it learns the direction back toward structure.

So the compass gets absorbed into the neural network.



Now we can write the training algorithm.

Choose a timestep t.

Take a sample from the cleaner previous state, which we can think of as x t minus one.

Add forward noise to obtain a noisy state y equals x t.

Then give y and t to the neural network.

The network predicts:

mu theta of y and t,

a cleaner state, one step back.

We compare this prediction to the true cleaner target using squared error.

At first, the prediction may be very wrong.

The loss meter is red.

The reverse arrow points in a suspicious direction.

Not ideal.

The model is basically saying, "I think the way home is...

through that wall."

But after many updates, the prediction improves.

The loss decreases.

The arrow becomes more aligned with the true reverse direction.

Repeat this over many samples and many noise levels, and the network learns a map of the probability terrain.

During sampling, we start from pure noise.

The model gives one small reverse step.

Then another.

Then another.

Each step is like a learned breadcrumb.

After many such steps, the noisy cloud moves back toward a structured data region.

Again, this is not an exact physical rewind of one original image.

It is a learned probabilistic path from noise toward high-probability data.

So far, however, every step has been discrete.

But what happens if we make the steps infinitely small?

The breadcrumbs become a continuous path.

And diffusion enters the world of stochastic differential equations.



We now take the discrete reverse steps and make them denser.

The gaps shrink.

The ticks on the time axis divide again and again: half, quarter, eighth, sixteenth.

Eventually, the separate breadcrumbs blur into a continuous curve.

When the steps become infinitesimally small,

the discrete noise term is replaced by a continuous random motion term:

d W.

This is the increment of a Wiener process.

The Wiener process is the standard mathematical model for continuous random motion.

It is the formal version of "this particle is jittering forever, and no, it is not just nervous."

A pure noise process can be written simply as:

d X equals square root beta of t, times d W.

At the level of individual particles, the paths look random.

Each particle wiggles in its own unpredictable way.

But now we change perspective.

Instead of following one particle, we follow the whole cloud.

As the particles spread, we group them into a histogram.

Then the histogram becomes a smooth density curve.

This is the key shift:

Individual sample paths are random.

The evolution of the probability density is structured.

And that density evolution is what the next equations describe.


To understand continuous diffusion, imagine a thin layer of oil floating on a slow stream.

Its motion has two separate components.

On the left side, we have drift.

Drift is deterministic.

The water current pushes the oil particles along a velocity field.

If the current curves, the particles curve.

If the current compresses, probability mass accumulates.

If it expands, probability spreads out.

Mathematically, drift looks like:

d X equals alpha of x and t, times d t.

On the right side, we have pure diffusion.

Even if the water is still, the oil particles do not stay perfectly fixed.

Random microscopic motion makes them spread.

They jitter, collide, and gradually move outward.

This random part is written as:

d X equals square root beta of t, times d W.

Now we merge the two halves.

The particles are carried by the flow, but also shaken by randomness.

Together, they form an Ito process:

d X equals alpha of x and t times d t,

plus square root beta of t times d W.

This is a compact way to say:

part deterministic motion,

part random diffusion,

all happening continuously in time.

It is like a river with a little statistical caffeine added.

And now the big question becomes:

If individual particles follow this process, how does the entire probability density move?


The answer is the Fokker-Planck equation.

SDEs describe how individual samples move.

Fokker-Planck describes how the whole probability density evolves.

In the animation, the drift term and the diffusion term are assembled into a single equation.

The density behaves like a fluid moving under a velocity field v:

partial p over partial t,

equals negative divergence of p times v.

This means that probability mass is flowing through space.

But now look at the velocity field:

v of x and t,

equals alpha of x and t,

minus beta of t over two,

times nabla log p of x and t.

And there it is again.

The score.

nabla log p of x and t.

The same score compass from the discrete story returns inside the continuous probability flow.

This is the bridge between the two worlds.

In discrete time, the score helped us step from noisy points toward higher probability.

In continuous time, the score becomes part of the velocity field that moves the entire probability density.

So Fokker-Planck is not just a terrifying equation with divergence symbols trying to ruin your afternoon.

It is the law of motion for probability itself.

It tells us how the cloud stretches, compresses, diffuses, and flows.

And if probability can flow forward, then naturally, we ask:

Can it flow backward?


In the forward direction, probability spreads outward.

Data becomes noise.

The vector field points away from structure.

The cloud becomes wider, softer, and less informative.

Now freeze time.

The arrows stop.

The noisy cloud is suspended.

And the score field appears.

The time-reversal theorem, associated with Brian Anderson's 1982 work, tells us something subtle and powerful:

Under suitable assumptions, the reverse process can also be described, in distribution, by another Ito process.

The phrase "in distribution" matters.

We are not physically rewinding every individual particle along the exact path it took.

That would be a little too much to ask, and also suspiciously close to time travel.

Instead, we are reversing the evolution of the probability distribution.

The reverse dynamics gain a score-dependent force.

This force counteracts the spreading caused by diffusion.

In the animation, the forward vector field fades.

The reverse arrows appear.

The cloud that was spreading outward begins to bend back inward.

The score acts like a steering force.

It turns the flow away from pure noise and back toward regions of high probability.

This is the continuous-time version of our earlier learned breadcrumbs.

One tiny motion at a time, the probability cloud is guided back toward structure.


All of this is beautiful on paper.

But computers do not take infinitely small steps.

Computers take finite steps, because a GPU also has a schedule and would like to go home eventually.

So to approximate a continuous trajectory, we need numerical solvers.

The simplest is Euler's method.

At the current point, look at the direction, then step forward.

y n plus one equals y n,

plus h times f of t n and y n.

The problem is that if the true curve bends, Euler can drift away quickly.

In the animation, the red path shoots off the ideal curve.

Euler is brave.

Euler is simple.

Euler is also occasionally overconfident.

Runge-Kutta is more careful.

RK4 sends out several slope estimates:

k one, k two, k three, and k four.

One looks at the beginning of the step.

Two look around the middle.

One looks near the end.

Then RK4 combines them:

y n plus one,

equals y n plus h over six,

times k one plus two k two plus two k three plus k four.

Instead of trusting one direction, it averages several informed guesses.

Visually, the RK4 path stays much closer to the ideal continuous trajectory than Euler.

In diffusion models, especially in probability-flow or ODE-based views,

numerical solvers like these approximate the path through time.

The neural network provides the learned score or vector field.

The solver decides how to step through it.

So the model learns the geometry.

The solver walks the geometry.

And the final sample is the result of both.


When everything works, diffusion feels almost magical.

Noise becomes structure.

A random cloud becomes an image.

A meaningless starting point becomes something that looks like it came from the data distribution.

But diffusion is not magic.

The score field is learned.

And learned fields can be wrong.

If the neural network gives a bad direction,

the numerical solver may follow it perfectly - and still go to the wrong place.

That is the terrifying part: a very accurate solver following a bad compass is still just confidently lost.

In the animation, one red arrow points the sample away from the data region.

The solver follows it.

The point leaves the safe probability terrain.

The final image becomes distorted, unstable, or strange.

These are failure cases.

They remind us that diffusion models are chains of approximations:

the reverse process is approximated,

the score is approximated,

the neural network is approximated,

and the continuous path is approximated by numerical steps.

And yet, when these approximations work together, the result is extraordinary.

From Markov chains to score functions, from MSE to neural networks, from Ito processes to Fokker-Planck equations,

the same idea keeps returning:

Structure can be recovered by following the geometry of probability.

Diffusion models do not physically reverse time.

They learn a probabilistic path from noise back toward data.

From noise to structure -

that is the mathematics of diffusion.

Thank you for watching.

Forward is designed.

Reverse is learned.

And probability shows the way.
